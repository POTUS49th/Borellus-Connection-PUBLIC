Somewhere in Cholon...

The boy is maybe nine. Old enough to run errands alone, young enough that the city still gives him reasons to stop and stare. He walks along a busy street with a paper-wrapped parcel tucked under one arm, weaving between scooters, bicycles, all other sorts of activity. The morning is already hot. Ahead of him, a French storefront sits between two Chinese businesses. It looks respectable in a tired way, not unlike other old French business in Saigon. The brass is polished, the shelves are dusty, and a noisy doorbell. The boy slows down as the door bangs open with a loud ding-ding. Two men are running out... not customers. Hongkongers. Young, hard-looking, one of them clutching something under his jacket. They don't look left or right as they run through traffic so fast a cyclist almost goes down trying to avoid them, and get on a black Citroën. It's pulling out from the curb before they have the doors shut, and it is gone a few seconds later. The boy stands there with the parcel under his arm, watching the car disappear through the street. Nothing happens as the boy steps closer to the open door and looks inside. Then, a bright flash. By nightfall, two Chinese men are found shot in an alley behind a gambling house.

"THE SEVEN DAYS OF BLOOD"

The seven days that follow are remembered later in Cholon as a bad week. A Corsican driver is pulled from his car near the river and beaten until her gives up three addresses, then is shot. A Cholon bookmaker disappears after dinner and turns up two days later in a drainage canal. A warehouse near Phu Lam burns for six hours. Emergency services arrive on time to keep the fire from spreading, but not in time to save anything or anyone inside. Then a French mechanic's assistant is shot in front of his wife because someone thinks he carries the wrong messages for the wrong men. Two RVNP patrolmen try to stop a car full of armed men and let it pass after one of them recognizes the passenger. A Chinese tea shop is wrecked with hand grenades after midnight. Three men are arrested in a police sweep and released before sunset. The newspapers call it criminal violence while the police call it an ongoing investigation. American officials call it local instability, the Chinese call it bad weather. The French simply call it regrettable. In private, everyone calls it what it is. Lim Boon Kiat was seriously injured in the shootout in the Continental. He survived, despite being described at one point as having been knocking on death's door. His daughter, Lim Be Gua, has been liaising with his lieutenants during his hospitalization. The instructions are clear: blood must be paid, but business must survive. For seven days, Cholon and the old French quarters trade bodies, fires, threats, and finally, messages. Then, as suddenly as it began, the violence stops. Not because the police win, nor because anyone forgives anything, but because money must flow. (edited)
